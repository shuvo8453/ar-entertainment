Filming in Bangladesh from the UK and USA: A Guide for Diaspora Filmmakers and International Co-Productions (2026)

A professional 3-day documentary shoot in Bangladesh using a full local crew through Libanza Films costs approximately GBP 3,500 to 8,000 (USD 4,500 to 10,000) all-inclusive — 60 to 75 percent less than an equivalent production in the UK or USA. 1. Who This Guide is for

This guide is written for three overlapping groups of filmmakers who want to produce content in Bangladesh from a UK or USA base.

Bangladeshi Diaspora Filmmakers

Second and third-generation Bangladeshi-British and Bangladeshi-American filmmakers returning to make a documentary, personal film or community story in Bangladesh. You may hold a British or American passport, a Bangladeshi passport, or dual nationality. You know the culture but you have never navigated the Bangladesh production system professionally.

Independent International Filmmakers

UK or USA-based documentary filmmakers without Bangladeshi heritage who have a story to tell in Bangladesh — climate, development, Rohingya crisis, RMG industry, urban Dhaka, culture or sport. You have made films elsewhere but this is your first Bangladesh production.

UK/USA Production Companies

Production companies based in the UK or USA who have commissioned a Bangladesh episode, series segment or co-production and need a trusted local production partner who can provide the production infrastructure, permits and on-ground management at international broadcast standard.

Libanza Films has worked directly with filmmakers and production companies in all three categories, including RagingNation Films (Los Angeles), Joshua Chronicles (USA), Crowd Productions (Belgium), Parc Film (Romania) and AFD France among others. The guide below reflects what these productions actually needed and what surprised them about the Bangladesh production process.

2. Permits — What You Need Even if You Have a Bangladeshi Passport

The single most common misconception among diaspora filmmakers is that a Bangladeshi passport removes the need for a filming permit. It does not. The Ministry of Information filming permit requirement is based on the nature of the production activity, not the passport you travel on.

Important: If you are using professional filming equipment (a cinema camera, dedicated documentary audio kit, professional lighting) and intending to distribute or broadcast the content, you need a Ministry of Information filming permit regardless of your passport. A Bangladeshi dual national travelling on a Bangladeshi passport conducting a professional production still needs MoI clearance for their project.

The passport difference that does matter

What your passport changes is the visa process, not the permit process. UK and USA passport holders require a tourist visa or journalist visa to enter Bangladesh. The journalist visa provides better protocol support for filming but is harder to obtain and requires sponsor letters. Most diaspora and independent filmmakers enter on a tourist visa and obtain their production credentials through the MoI permit process, which is managed by their local production partner (Libanza Films).

4. Finding and Working With a Local Production Partner

For most diaspora and international filmmakers, a trusted local production partner is the difference between a production that runs smoothly and one that wastes half the shoot days on logistics problems. Here is what the right production partner relationship looks like, and what to be cautious about.

What a good local production partner does differently

  • Tells you what is not possible as clearly as what is. Overconfident fixers who say yes to everything are the leading cause of failed Bangladesh productions for international filmmakers.
  • Manages the permit process proactively with status updates, not reactively when you ask. You should receive confirmation that the MoI application was submitted within 48 hours of signing the contract.
  • Scouts locations in advance and shares a written location scout report with photographs before you arrive. Arriving in Bangladesh and scouting locations on shoot day is expensive and exhausting.
  • Has established relationships with the local authorities and communities you will be filming. In Bangladesh, the relationship the fixer holds with a community or official is often more important than the permit document.
  • Understands the editorial intent of the production and can flag when a planned approach will create access problems — for example, certain framing choices or sensitive subject areas that require additional care in Bangladesh's political and social context.

Using relatives as fixers — the diaspora-specific risk

Many diaspora filmmakers assume they can use their own family contacts in Bangladesh as a cost-saving alternative to a professional fixer. For purely personal, mobile-phone content this can work. For a professional documentary production it almost always creates problems. Your uncle in Sylhet may have excellent community relationships but no experience managing a Ministry of Information permit application, no knowledge of CAAB drone permit requirements, no professional crew contacts and no experience managing a shoot day schedule against a production brief. The permit issue alone — where a mistake or delay has legal and commercial consequences — requires a professional who has done it before.

5. The Specific Challenges Diaspora Filmmakers Face in Bangladesh

Diaspora filmmakers face a different set of challenges to pure international crews. Cultural familiarity can create blind spots that a fully international crew avoids through extra caution. Here are the challenges specific to the diaspora filmmaker experience.

The "I know Bangladesh" assumption

Diaspora filmmakers who grew up visiting Bangladesh often underestimate how much has changed, particularly around filming regulations, political sensitivity and community consent expectations. The Bangladesh production landscape in 2026 is significantly more regulated than it was in 2015. Assuming family knowledge is equivalent to production knowledge is a risk. Libanza Films provides a pre-production briefing covering current sensitivities for every production, regardless of the filmmaker's background.

Community access expectations

In Bangladesh, a person with Bangladeshi heritage arriving with a camera can be expected by communities to be producing content that represents them positively, or that will help their community abroad. Managing these expectations when the film has a different editorial purpose requires careful local liaison. A professional local production partner manages this community communication before the camera arrives.

Subject safety and consent

Bangladesh has specific sensitivities around content that depicts poverty, working conditions, political themes or minority community life. Content that is standard documentary practice in the UK or USA — vox pops with workers, filming inside a factory, street interviews — can create safety risks for subjects in Bangladesh if the content is distributed internationally without appropriate consent and context management. Libanza Films advises on subject consent protocols for every production type.

Equipment clearance at customs

Diaspora filmmakers travelling on a Bangladeshi passport and bringing professional filming equipment from the UK or USA face closer customs scrutiny than tourist travellers. A cinema camera, audio kit and lighting entering on a Bangladeshi passport is more likely to attract customs interest than the same equipment entering on a British passport as declared professional goods. Prepare a full customs declaration pack for all professional equipment regardless of which passport you travel on.
